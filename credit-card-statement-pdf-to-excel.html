Credit card statement PDF to Excel: convert it and check the total

Card statements are harder to convert than bank statements. They often split purchases, payments and fees into separate sections, print the amounts without a minus sign, and rarely show a balance after each line. Here's how to get one into a clean spreadsheet and prove nothing went missing.

Before you start: download the CSV if you can

Most card issuers let you download recent activity as CSV or Excel from the online account, usually under "Activity", "Transactions" or "Statements". If the period you need is still available there, use that file. The PDF route is for older statements, closed cards, or statements someone sent you.

Getting the rows out of the PDF

  1. Excel (Microsoft 365, Windows): Data → Get Data → From File → From PDF. Pick the table that holds the transactions on each page and use Append Queries to stack them. Card statements often have one table for payments and another for purchases, so you may need to append both.
  2. Tabula (free, Windows/Mac/Linux): draw a box around the transaction area on each page and export to CSV.
  3. Copy and paste works for a single short statement if you then use Data → Text to Columns. Expect to fix the descriptions by hand.

None of these read scanned paper statements. Those need OCR, and OCR mistakes in amounts are exactly what the check below catches.

Fix the signs

On a bank statement, money out is negative. On a card statement, a purchase is usually printed as a plain positive number because it increases what you owe, and payments or refunds appear with a minus sign or a "CR" after them. Decide which view you want before you go further:

  • "What I owe" view (matches the statement): purchases, fees and interest positive; payments and refunds negative.
  • "My spending" view (what budgeting and accounting apps expect): purchases negative, payments positive. Multiply the amount column by −1: type -1 in a spare cell, copy it, select the amounts and use Paste Special → Multiply.

Turn "CR" amounts into numbers first. With the text in A2, =IF(RIGHT(TRIM(A2),2)="CR",-VALUE(SUBSTITUTE(A2,"CR","")),VALUE(A2)) gives you a signed value in the "what I owe" view.

The check: previous balance to new balance

The summary box at the top of a card statement gives you everything you need. In the "what I owe" view, with your signed amounts in column C:

= previous_balance + SUM(C:C) - new_balance     → should be 0.00

If it's not zero, compare section totals. Most statements print a subtotal for payments, purchases, fees and interest. Sum the same rows in your sheet with SUMIF on a category column, and the section that disagrees is where a row was dropped, doubled or misread. Common culprits:

  • Interest and fees printed in a separate box that the table extraction skipped.
  • Foreign currency purchases where the original amount and the converted amount both landed in the amount column.
  • A payment whose "CR" marker ended up in the description, so it was counted as a purchase.
  • Rows split across a page break.

Dates

Card statements often show two dates per line, the transaction date and the posting date. Keep one (the posting date is what the issuer used to build the statement) and make sure Excel reads it as a date. Many card statements print dates without a year, like 03/14. Add the year from the statement period, and watch out for a December statement that includes January rows, or the other way round.
